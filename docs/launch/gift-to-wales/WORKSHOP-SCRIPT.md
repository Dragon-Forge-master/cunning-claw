# Workshop: Using AI safely in your small business

A one-hour session for Chris to run at a library, a Business Wales event, an
FSB Wales or chamber of commerce meeting. The slides are
[`workshop-slides.pdf`](workshop-slides.pdf) (16, 16:9, built from
`workshop-slides.html`), the handout is
[`workshop-handout.pdf`](workshop-handout.pdf) (one A4 page, print one per
person plus spares), and the demo is in
[DEMO-CHECKLIST.md](DEMO-CHECKLIST.md), which is the part to rehearse.

**Who it is for:** owners and staff of small firms, sole traders and anyone
using AI tools at work. No technical knowledge assumed.

**What they leave with:** four habits that apply to any AI assistant, three
questions to ask before pressing yes, what to do with an email that gives
orders, and, if they want it, a free way to start.

**The shape:** the habits are about AI assistants in general. Cunning Claw is
the demo because it is the one Chris knows inside out, and he says so. If a
host allows no product promotion, skip slide 13 (start free) and keep the
QR code on slide 15 only if the host agrees; the talk still stands. Slide 16
(Learn mode) is an optional extra about something **not released**: show it
only if there is time and interest, and say plainly that it is planned.

## The hour, minute by minute

Times are from the start. The bold lines are what to say, near enough; the
rest is what to do.

### 0:00–0:04 · Slide 1 · Welcome

Introduce yourself in a sentence: Chris, from Dragon Forge AI in Cardiff, and
you build an AI assistant. **"Today is about using any AI tool safely. I'll
demo mine because it's the one I know, and I'll tell you what it can't do as
well as what it can. It's free, and you don't need to buy anything."**

### 0:04–0:06 · Slide 2 · The hour

Read the six lines. Ask people to hold questions for the end, except "I
didn't follow that", which is always welcome.

### 0:06–0:10 · Slide 3 · From answering to doing

Ask for a show of hands: who has used ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini? Who has
let one send something for them? **"Answering is one thing. An assistant that
can send, pay or post for you needs rules about when."**

### 0:10–0:15 · Slide 4 · The new trick

Read the quote aloud. Ask: **"If a temp started on Monday and found this note
in the post, what would you want them to do?"** Take two answers. The point:
you would want them to bring it to you, not act on it. An AI assistant reads
everything as words, and cannot always tell yours from a stranger's.

### 0:15–0:27 · Slides 5 to 8 · Four habits (three minutes each)

- **Slide 5, outside words are information.** Example: a supplier's PDF can
  carry hidden text. Ask of any tool: how does it treat what it reads?
- **Slide 6, sends, spends, publishes wait for a yes.** **"The person
  reading the request is the real safeguard. If you click yes without
  reading, no software can save you."** Silence should mean no.
- **Slide 7, know where your words go.** Most AI runs on someone else's
  computers. Google's free plan: Google may use what you send to improve its
  products. Customers' personal details: a paid plan, and the ICO's guidance.
  **Do not give legal advice; point to the ICO.**
- **Slide 8, fewest keys.** Start with reading and drafting. Never banking
  passwords. A separate account where you can. Backups.

### 0:27–0:39 · Slide 9, then the live demo

Read slide 9, then switch to the laptop and follow **Part D** of
[DEMO-CHECKLIST.md](DEMO-CHECKLIST.md). Show the email first, so the room
has read it before the assistant does. Narrate what you see as it happens:
the amber "instructions inside" flag, the reply, the refusal, the card.
Then Part E (the box) if you are on Linux and there is time; skip it
otherwise.

If the assistant does something you did not expect, say so. **"That's the
AI's judgement, and it isn't perfect. That's exactly why the card is there."**
Never cover for it.

### 0:39–0:42 · Slide 10 · What you just saw, and who did it

Go down the table. The first four rows are the code: they happen the same
way every time. The last row is the AI's judgement: usually right, not
always. That difference is the whole talk.

### 0:42–0:48 · Slides 11 and 12 · Reading a card, and your turn

Slide 11: the three questions. Slide 12: read each line and ask for hands,
"wait for your yes, or not?" The answers are on the handout:

1. Draft a reply: no yes needed yet, because a draft waits for you anyway.
2. Send that reply: yes first.
3. Read a supplier's price list: fine, as information; don't follow orders inside it.
4. Change bank details because an email says so: yes first, **and phone the
   supplier on a number you already had.** This is the one that costs firms
   money; spend the extra minute on it.

### 0:48–0:52 · Slide 13 · Free to download and start

Skip if the host asked for no product promotion. Otherwise: three steps, and
read the cost box aloud, all of it. **"Free to download and start, with a
free Google key. The AI brain is a separate service; past Google's free
allowance, or for private work, you pay that service, not me."**

### 0:52–0:54 · Slide 14 · The honest bit

Read it as written. Do not soften it.

### 0:54–1:00 · Slide 15 · Take home, and questions

Hand out the handout if not already given. Take questions. Leave slide 15 up
during questions so people can scan the QR code. Slide 16 only if asked about
what's next, and say it is planned, not released.

## Likely questions, and true answers

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Is it safe?" | "Safer than letting an AI act without asking, and honest about where it stops. It's new and nothing about it has been independently audited. The card is the real boundary, so read it." Never "safe", "secure" or "unhackable" without that. |
| "Is it really free?" | "Free to download and start, with a free Google key. Google's free allowance has daily limits; more use, or a paid key for private work, is paid to the AI service." Never "completely free". |
| "Does Google see my emails?" | "Whatever you ask it about goes to the AI service whose key you use. On Google's free plan, Google may use it to improve its products. For customers' details, use a paid key. Or run a model on your own computer, so nothing leaves it; small local models are weaker at spotting tricks, and it warns you when one reads outside text." |
| "Does it work on Windows / Mac?" | "Yes, there are downloads for both. The safety box for its commands is on Linux; on Mac it's built but not yet tested on a real Mac; Windows has none yet, and it tells you so on every command." |
| "What happens when the free allowance runs out?" | "It says so in plain words. The daily allowance comes back at midnight Pacific time, which is 8 am in the UK." |
| "Does it speak Welsh?" | "Its screens are in English. Its scheduler understands Welsh day names, and the check for planted instructions knows Welsh. The AI brain may reply in Welsh if you write in Welsh; that depends on the AI service." |
| "Is it GDPR-compliant?" | "I can't certify that, and I won't claim it. For personal data, use a paid key and read the ICO's guidance on AI." Name no standard as met. |
| "What's the catch? Who pays you?" | "It's MIT-licensed, so every released version stays free for good. A paid plan with no keys to set up is planned, and it isn't available yet. There's no crypto token, and there never will be." |
| "Can it stop all scams?" | "No. The check that flags planted instructions is a set of rules, and rules can be got round. That's why anything that sends, spends or publishes waits for you." |

## Words not to use

From [`../v0.3/MARKETING-PLAN.md`](../v0.3/MARKETING-PLAN.md), "What we never
say", which governs here too: no "never", "guaranteed", "unhackable",
"completely free", "production-ready"; no standard named as met; no claim
that the scanner stops prompt injection or that the sandbox protects files;
no "sandboxed on Mac" without "not yet tested on a real Mac"; nothing implying
Windows has a box. No statistic, customer, award or partnership we do not
have.
