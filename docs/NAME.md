# Where the name comes from

**Cunning Claw** is not a fantasy flourish. *Cunning folk* is a real occupational term
from British history, and Wales had its own distinct version of it.

## Dyn hysbys — "the knowing man"

The Welsh cunning folk were the **dynion hysbys**, "the knowing ones" — singular
*dyn hysbys*, literally *the knowing man*. They were the Welsh counterparts to the
cunning folk found across Europe, and they operated in villages across Wales into the
twentieth century.

Elsewhere in Britain they went by wise men and wise women, pellars, wizards, conjurers.
"White witch" was mostly a label applied from outside, by clergy and educated observers
who wanted to lump the whole business together — not what practitioners called themselves.

## They were tradespeople, not mystics

This is the part worth building on. The historical record is clear that cunning folk
**were not spiritual seekers pursuing personal transformation — they were tradespeople,
and their trade was solving specific problems for paying customers.**

You went to a *dyn hysbys* with a job: find who stole the horse, break a curse laid on the
herd, read the weather for a sailing, treat a sick child, settle whether a marriage was
wise. He worked, and you paid him. Service work, at a price, for people with a problem.

That is exactly the right posture for this software. Not a companion, not a chat partner,
not a personality to be enjoyed for its own sake. **A practitioner you bring a job to.**

## The Welsh lineage is real and local

The most famous were **John Harries and his son Henry, of Cwrt-y-Cadno in
Carmarthenshire** — an hour and a half up the road from Cardiff. John Harries (Shon Harri
Shon, c.1785–1839) was baptised at Caeo in 1785.

He was not a village eccentric. He had a formal education, kept the major medical texts of
his day alongside Latin and Greek, and practised as a physician and astrologer. The family
were known across Wales and over the English border as serious medical practitioners and
skilled astrologers who held real standing.

Their working papers survive at the **National Library of Wales** — patient prescriptions
and payment records, draft horoscopes worked up on request, an annotated astrological
almanac, patient correspondence, lecture notes, medical treatises.

Read that list again: a practitioner keeping records of the work, the reasoning behind each
judgement, and who asked for what. It is a case file. It is closer to what this software
does — memory, journal, skills as written procedure — than anything in the Marvel canon.

## What the tradition actually offers the product

- **The claim in the name is knowledge, not obedience.** *Hysbys* is "knowing". A cunning
  man was hired for judgement, not compliance.
- **Trade, not mysticism.** Bring it a job. It does the job. It says what it did.
- **Two things at once.** The Harrieses were doctors *and* astrologers — practical craft
  next to something stranger. Local shell commands next to a frontier model is the same
  shape.
- **Cunning cuts both ways.** Sharp enough to help you, sharp enough to spot a con. A
  cunning man's stock in trade included telling clients when they were being had — which is
  precisely what this thing does when a web page tries to give it orders.

## A note on how it ended

It was not the witch trials or the church that finished the cunning folk. It was **the
modern state** — professional medicine, professional policing, the insurance claim.
The trade did not lose an argument; the world reorganised around it.

Worth knowing, and worth not being precious about. The name is a lineage, not a costume.
