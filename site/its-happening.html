Cunning Claw / It's happening now

Attacks on AI assistants are happening now.

For two years, prompt injection was a warning in research papers: hide an instruction in an email or a web page, and an AI assistant reading it might obey. This autumn, security researchers showed one email taking over an AI agent, and a security company found phishing emails written for the reader's AI as well as the reader. Here is what happened, what the UK's National Cyber Security Centre says about it, and what Cunning Claw does about it.

Where this comes from

Every fact below is from the original reports, read on 24 November 2026 and linked at the foot of the page. Where a report described a test rather than a real victim, we say so. We are not connected to any of the companies named.

October 2026 · Salt Labs

One email was enough to take over an AI agent.

Researchers at Salt Labs, part of Salt Security, connected a test Gmail account to Manus, an AI agent that can read your inbox and act for you. Then they sent that account a single email.

The person only had to ask Manus to check their inbox. A plain instruction in the email was caught by Manus's safety check. A version disguised with an obscure JavaScript encoding was not: Manus ran code to "decode" the message, which ran the attacker's code, and its safety warning came only after the code had run.

Inside the agent's workspace, the researchers found the access token for the user's Gmail. When other services such as Google Drive or GitHub had been connected, their credentials were there too. Salt reported it through Meta's bug bounty programme, and it has been fixed: Salt says it is no longer exploitable.

No customer was harmed; this was a test account. The point is how little it took: no link to click, no attachment to open, just an assistant doing what it was asked.

October 2026 · Barracuda

Phishing that talks to your assistant as well as to you.

Barracuda, an email security company, found phishing emails aimed at two readers at once: a person, and the AI assistant that summarises their inbox. The person gets the usual bait. The assistant gets instructions the person cannot see.

Barracuda lists four ways the instructions were hidden: inside HTML comments, as invisible text (a zero-size font, or white on white), in Base64-encoded data, and in zero-width characters. The aim was to make the assistant's summary call the email legitimate or urgent.

One example Barracuda saw in real attacks will be familiar to anyone who has paid a supplier: an invoice email with a hidden block telling the summarising AI to add a fake "priority action" changing the supplier's payment details.

Among its advice: require a person's approval for payments, supplier changes and other sensitive decisions. Malwarebytes, reporting the same research, adds the oldest rule there is: check any change of bank details through a separate channel you already trust.

The UK's view

It cannot simply be patched.

The National Cyber Security Centre has been blunt about this. In its December 2025 blog post Prompt injection is not SQL injection (it may be worse), it says there's a good chance prompt injection will never be properly mitigated the way older attacks were.

Its advice is to stop relying on the AI to spot the trick, and to build deterministic (non-LLM) safeguards that constrain the actions of the system: limit what an AI reading strangers' text is allowed to do. And it warns: Beware any that claim they can 'stop' prompt injection.

We agree, and we do not claim it. What follows is how Cunning Claw is built on that advice.

What Cunning Claw does about it

Strangers' words are data, never orders.

Labelled as data

Fenced off

Every email, web page and message it reads reaches the AI marked as outside text, with the standing rule that outside text gives no orders.

A second look

Hidden tricks shown up

Before the AI sees outside text, characters a person cannot see are stripped out. A scanner looks for instructions aimed at an assistant, including Base64 that decodes into orders, and flags the page as having instructions inside.

A person decides

The card waits for you

Sending, spending, publishing, deleting and running commands wait for your yes on an approval card. Once outside text has tried to give orders, standing permissions are suspended and every card says so.

Keys out of reach

Nothing to steal in the box

On Linux its shell runs in a box where your SSH keys, sign-ins and browser profiles are replaced by empty stand-ins, and its own API keys are kept out of the command's reach. Windows has no such box yet; on macOS it is untested.

Our first film shows the supplier version of this attack, for real: a page hiding a line, two pixels high, telling AI assistants to switch the bank details and say nothing. The scanner flagged it, and Cunning Claw gave the real details and told the owner about the planted ones. Watch it (76 seconds).

What we do not promise

Cunning Claw can still be fooled; the scanner is a heuristic, and anyone can read how it works. The approval card is the real boundary, and it only works if you read it. Cunning Claw is new, has run on very few machines, and has not been independently audited. How it is secured states each limit beside each guarantee.

Whatever assistant you use

Four things to do today.

  • Check any change of bank details by phone, on a number you already had, never one in the email. An AI summary saying it is genuine proves nothing.
  • Give an assistant the least it needs. An AI that reads your email should not also be able to send, pay or delete without asking you first.
  • Read the approval before you tap it. If you cannot tell what you are agreeing to, the answer is no.
  • Report phishing to the NCSC by forwarding it to report@phishing.gov.uk.
